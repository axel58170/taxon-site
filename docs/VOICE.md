# Editorial voice

This guide describes how good Taxon Bridge copy sounds. Follow
[CONTENT_GUIDELINES.md](CONTENT_GUIDELINES.md) for the hard content rules.

## Plain, concrete, and knowledgeable

Write about species, animals, plants, fungi, names, languages, and situations
people recognise. The reader may know much more about birds, biology, taxonomy,
or languages than about software. Be knowledgeable without sounding technical,
and concise without becoming vague.

Make the person, species, or name the subject. Discuss the software or its
architecture only when the reader needs that information to do something.

Prefer ordinary verbs: **find, see, look up, read, select, enter, open, close,**
and **continue**. Avoid generic marketing claims and unsupported adjectives such
as **seamless, effortless, powerful,** and **complete**.

## Start with one real situation

Begin with something the reader can immediately recognise:

> See a bird name in French and want to know the Dutch or English name?

Do not begin with an audience label, product capability, workflow, or abstract
benefit. Instead of “Ideal for birders, students and translators,” develop each
important audience as its own short story based on something those people
actually need to do.

Keep one situation in each section. Local bird reports, Merlin, personal lists,
and Big Year birding may all be relevant, but combining them makes the point
harder to follow.

## Describe the goal, then the mechanism

Name the thing the person wants to accomplish:

> Look up a species while reading.

Do not frame the feature as a “Share Sheet workflow.” Mention **Share Sheet**
only when the reader needs that interface label to perform a step.

Use the real-world object for the goal: **species, bird, animal, plant,** or
**fungus**. Use **name** for text the person encounters, enters, or selects:

> Select its name and send it to Taxon Bridge.

Use **taxon** only where the scientific distinction matters to the reader.

## Talk about languages naturally

When people want to know what a species is called across languages,
**translations** usually matches their mental model:

> The translations appear straight away.

Use **name** for a particular common or scientific name, its source, or the text
someone interacts with. Preserve scientific accuracy without forcing internal
terminology into ordinary prose.

Use these established terms consistently:

- **selected languages**, not “chosen languages”;
- **pop-up**, not “result,” for the interface shown temporarily over another
  app;
- **Recent Searches**, not “Recent Taxa”;
- **look up** for finding a species or its translations.

## Let examples carry the meaning

A concrete example can explain the product better than an abstract claim. For
example, showing that `guêpier`, `bee-eater`, and `bijeneter` refer to the same
bird may make the point without an additional explanation about a “complete
multilingual picture.”

Be cautious with language such as **workflow, identity, context, capability,
complete view, multilingual picture,** and **point of reference**. These words
often describe the system's perspective rather than the person's.

Once an example or sentence has made the point, stop. Do not repeat the benefit
to introduce secondary information. Add details where they become useful.

## Delete filler; explain useful limitations

Remove sentences that repeat a benefit, introduce an undeveloped scenario, or
add promotion without information. Avoid constructions such as “Less time doing
X, more time doing Y.”

Do not organise normal feature copy around limitations. If a limitation changes
what the person must do, state it plainly and give the next step. Apple Books,
for example, needs a Shortcut because it does not provide the same Share Sheet
route. Do not explain or apologise for implementation details that do not help
the reader continue.

## Preserve reviewed wording

When implementing approved copy, reproduce it exactly unless an edit is
explicitly requested. Do not expand it, add transitions, or re-market it while
changing layout or code. Keep editorial revision and implementation separate
when practical.

## Quick review

Before accepting customer-facing copy, ask:

- Can the reader understand the point on the first read?
- Is the section about one recognisable situation?
- Is the subject the person, species, or name rather than an internal mechanism?
- Could an example replace an abstract explanation?
- Does every sentence add information?
- Would deleting a sentence make the copy clearer?
- Is technical terminology present because the reader needs it?
